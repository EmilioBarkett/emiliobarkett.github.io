## Introduction

During the Industrial Revolution, the social sciences emerged specifically to make sense of a disruption reshaping work, institutions, and social life. It was a transformation so disruptive that people built entirely new frameworks to explain it. But these frameworks arrived after the fact. AI presents a disruption of comparable magnitude to work, institutions, and social structure, but on a far more rapid timeline, and a social science that is largely reactive may not be sufficient. Society needs people thinking about AI’s effects now, while there is time to shape the transition rather than just explain it after the fact.[^1] Yet within the AI safety ecosystem, social scientists remain one of the most underused sources of talent.

At present, social scientists do not have a consistently legible path into the AI safety ecosystem (AIS). They do have two weak alternatives:

* **Graduate school.** AIS-literate advisors within the social sciences are rare, and pursuing a technical degree can mean being out of depth technically.  
* **Existing fellowships.** Generally, they are split between technical and policy/governance tracks, with social scientists pushed to the latter.

Neither path offers a structured, reliable path for a social scientist interested in contributing to AIS. A frequent consequence of this gap is for talented researchers settling for something below their expectations or worse, abandoning AIS altogether.

To address this gap, I’m proposing Societal Impacts Research on AI (SIRA), a mentored, 12 week research fellowship pairing social scientists across career stages with mentors already working at the intersection of the social sciences and AIS. The goal is to give this talent pool a structured path in, and to give the field research and researchers it has no consistent way to access.

The present doc is being circulated to organizers of existing AIS fellowships to gather feedback on the viability of the idea and potential partnerships to host this new research stream. ## Mission Statement

SIRA exists to give social scientists a structured path to do research in the AI safety ecosystem. We aim to identify talented researchers across career stages, from undergraduates to postdocs, support their development through mentored research, and connect them with careers across research organizations and labs, so the field gains the social-scientific methods and questions it currently lacks.

## [Theory of Change](#theory-of-change) {#theory-of-change}

If social scientists are given a structured path into AIS through mentored research, then they will produce relevant AIS research during the program and successfully transition into impactful careers in the field.

* **Inputs:** Experienced mentors at the intersection of the social sciences and AIS; motivated social scientists across career stages; a clear application pipeline (potentially partnered with an existing organization); program funding to cover stipends, housing, and research support.  
* **Activities:** Matching fellows with expert mentors, running the research fellowship, and providing career placement support.  
* **Outputs:** A cohort of 15-20 social scientists trained, total hours of mentorship completed, and a portfolio of finished research projects.  
* **Short-Term Outcomes:** Fellows produce viable AIS research during the program while gaining field-specific skills and a deep understanding of the broader ecosystem.  
* **Medium-Term Outcomes:** Fellows successfully transition and launch careers within the AIS ecosystem.  
* **Long-Term Outcomes:** The broader AIS ecosystem is enriched by social science methodologies and novel research questions, leading to more comprehensive safety solutions.

### Key Assumptions to Test

* **Demand:** There are enough AIS mentors who have the time and desire to guide social scientists.  
* **Job Market:** The AIS field has open career opportunities and a willingness to hire people with social science backgrounds.  
* **Comparative Advantage:** Technical AI researchers cannot simply substitute for social scientists. True social science requires distinct tacit knowledge and disciplinary cultures that technical researchers, even with AI assistance, cannot easily replicate.  
* **Feasibility:** Social scientists can adapt to the technical context of AIS quickly enough to produce research *during* the fellowship.  
* **Scale Ambiguity:** It is unclear whether AIS needs a small handful of highly specialized social scientists or hundreds of them, making the ideal program size difficult to determine.  
* **Partnership Strategy:** Integrate social science tracks into existing fellowships, rather than creating a new fellowship.

## The Problem

Within the AI safety ecosystem, social scientists remain one of the most underused sources of talent. This is surprising, given that since the Industrial Revolution, social scientists have made sense of disruptions to work, institutions, and social life, and the emergence of AI has already exceeded those prior disruptions. Unlike at the start of the Industrial Revolution, when the social sciences had yet to emerge in response to it, a deep talent pool of researchers already exists today, whose contributions could help understand the societal impacts of AI and how to navigate the future.

I believe that the AI safety ecosystem would benefit from tapping into the social science talent pool.[^3]

At present, social scientists do not have a consistently legible path into the AI safety ecosystem (AIS), except for two weak alternatives:

### Graduate School

For an undergraduate from the social sciences, graduate school offers a natural path to pursue. For our purposes, I focus on PhD programs and have identified two sub-paths:[^4]

#### PhD in the Social Sciences

Pursuing a PhD in the social sciences has a limited number of advisors broadly interested in AI[^5] and an even smaller number interested in AIS. Thus, one is constrained by the bounded number of options. It is possible to down-weight research alignment with an advisor, but risk spending time pursuing a non-satisfying path, likely outside of AIS. The rarer alternative is to find the ‘unicorn’ advisor in the social sciences who is interested in AIS.[^6] If you can do that, do that\![^7]

#### PhD Outside the Social Sciences

Pursuing a PhD outside the social sciences and in a technical domain, usually limits options to CS departments. This path greatly increases the number of advisors who are interested in AI and AIS. However, a social scientist may experience discomfort due to their lack of technical depth or be viewed as the “expert social scientist” in the department, when they have yet to develop these skills.[^8] Of the two sub-paths within graduate school, this is probably the better choice, given the exposure to AI through a CS department and the upward mobility available as being the “rare” social scientist.

### Fellowships

Fellowships offer a robust way to gain direct research experience, network, and acquire context within AIS. Most fellowships are split between technical and policy/governance tracks. Social scientists tend to be grouped to the latter by default. Despite a long list of fellowships in varying formats, few cater to social scientists,[^9] and with no clear path, many settle for something outside their expectations, or worse, abandon AIS altogether.

## The Solution: Societal Impacts Research on AI (SIRA)

To give social scientists a legible path into AIS, I'm proposing a fellowship: **Societal Impacts Research on AI (SIRA)**.

### Format

The fellowship would run for 10-12 weeks, with a cohort of 15-20 fellows.

### Mentorship Structure

Mentors will be sourced first, from both academia (PhDs, postdocs, faculty already doing AIS-adjacent work) and industry (e.g., Anthropic's Societal Impacts team, OpenAI’s Economics Team). Mentees apply second, once mentors are confirmed.[^10]

Matching will happen two ways. (1) mentors can offer specific projects that mentees apply directly to (useful for less experienced mentees like undergrads who would benefit from a defined starting point), and (2) mentors can offer a general interest direction where mentees will be matched by fit (useful for more experienced mentees like PhD students or postdocs who could bring their own research interests). 

For a list of questions that fellows could work on, see [Illustrative Research Questions](#illustrative-research-questions).

The fellowship is built around several structural supports that existing fellowships (e.g., MATS, Pivotal, ERA:AI) have found helpful to turn mentorship into actual research output:

* **Research Management.** Distinct from the mentor, a research manager helps fellows scope a project that is feasible within 10-12 weeks and helps them stay on track throughout the process.  
* **Seminars, workshops, and guest speakers.** Spanning both technical AIS and the social sciences, fellows will be exposed to the AIS ecosystem, gaining context and specificity beyond their mentor’s specialty.  
* **In-person research environment.** We aim to host in-person (see [Location](#structure-and-location)), given that daily proximity to other fellows is a cornerstone of what makes short programs like this work.[^11]  
* **Stipend, research funding, housing, and travel support.** We aim to remove financial barriers that would otherwise make this kind of program inaccessible.  
* **Concluding symposium.** Fellows will present their work to an audience of mentors and the broader AIS ecosystem. 

### Structure and Location {#structure-and-location}

Structurally, the fellowship can take one of two forms: (1) a standalone fellowship with its own identity, admissions, and operations, or (2) appended to an existing fellowship (e.g., MATS, Pivotal, ERA:AI), run as a dedicated research stream, inheriting admissions infrastructure, mentor networks, and credibility with funders.

Depending on what structure the fellowship takes on, the location could vary. Below are several locations that the fellowship could independently run at or be appended to.

* **NYC**, hosted at [Collider](https://collider.nyc/) (standalone): NYC does not pull strongly for technical AIS talent, which could make it a strong fit for non-technical talent like social scientists. However, the AIS culture there is correspondingly weaker than in SF or London.  
* **Boston**, hosted at [CBAI](https://www.cbai.ai/) (append option): Similar reasoning to NYC on both talent and culture, but with the option to run as a stream inside CBAI’s existing program rather than standing up something new.  
* **SF**, hosted at [Constellation](https://constellation.org/), likely via [MATS](https://www.matsprogram.org/) (append option): Talent and AIS culture here are both strong. However, a social science focused program risks being buried or not taken seriously alongside the technical programs it would share space with.  
* **London**, hosted at [LISA](https://www.safeai.org.uk/), likely via [Pivotal](https://www.pivotal-research.org/) (append option): same tradeoff as SF, strong culture, but a strong pull toward technical and policy work.  
* **Remote/in-person**, hosted via [Kairos](https://kairos-project.org/) (append / standalone option): Kairos operates fully remotely, but are expanding into physical spaces via [Generator](https://generatorresidency.org/) and [Global Challenges Project](https://www.globalchallengesproject.org/).

I would prefer appending to an existing fellowship as I believe this offers the cleanest path to test its viability. I believe that MATS is the strongest candidate for an in-person fellowship, given their recent expansion of research streams and general openness to explore new ideas.[^12] Next, I would be excited to work with Kairos in exploring a fully-remote or in-person standalone fellowship, given their strong interest in exploring new ways to make meaningful impact within AI safety.[^13]

### Funding

Regardless of the structure SIRA takes on (i.e., standalone or appended), the funding should be externally raised, likely through [Coefficient Giving](https://coefficientgiving.org/).

For our purposes here, I focus on costs associated with an in-person fellowship.

Running an inaugural cohort of 15-20 fellows costs roughly $49,500 per fellow in direct support. Additional costs will include program-level overhead. Per-fellow direct costs include (1) a living stipend \~$12,000-$18,000, (2) compute and experiment allocation \~$6,000-$10,000 per month, (3) housing support \~$3,000 per month, and (4) travel support to and from host city \~$1,500. All-in, per-fellow direct costs will be \~$740,000.[^14]

Program-level costs include (1) dedicated research management staffing, (2) cohort programming (seminars, workshops, guest lectures), (3) a closing symposium, and (4) general operations. Program-level costs scale with structural infrastructure, and would be most extreme running independently.

A high level breakdown of total costs can be found in the [Funding Table](#funding-table).

### Timeline to Launch

We are targeting launch for Summer 2027\.

Working backwards, the two options described in [Structure and Location](#structure-and-location) require different paths. An independent fellowship means building admissions, fundraising, operations, and space from nothing, all of which need lead time well before applications can open, likely putting the critical planning window in mid-to-late 2026\. An appended fellowship means folding SIRA into an existing program’s cycle as a new stream, which shortens the operational runway considerably but depends on securing a host organization’s buy-in and aligning with their existing cohort calendar.

### Outcomes

Outcomes fall into four tiers, roughly ordered by how directly they translate into value for AIS as a field, moving from immediate, object-level effects toward slower, compounding ones.

Direct Outcomes

* Fellows matriculate into full-time AIS roles.  
* Mentee-mentor pairs surface and correct blind spots in existing technical or governance research.  
* The fellowship produces research findings during the program, usable once published.

Organizational Outcomes

* AIS organizations gain a vetted talent pipeline, lowering the cost and risk of hiring social scientists who currently have no clear AIS-readiness signal.

Field-Building Outcomes

* Social science talent gets pulled into AIS more broadly, with spillover benefits across the field.  
* A durable peer network begins to form among fellows.  
* Successful fellows return as mentors in future cohorts, or go on to strengthen applications to other fellowships or PhD programs.  
* A successful pilot becomes proof that can justify permanent institutional structures, like  a standing research stream.

Awareness and Legitimacy Outcomes

* Fellows already in academia evangelize for AIS among their social science cohorts.  
* Fellows publish papers at AIS conferences, building broader field awareness of the need for more social scientists.

## Why This, Why Now

### The case that this matters now, not just eventually

Within the context of societal impacts research, timelines are an important consideration. At first glance, a fellowship like this can look like a long-horizon bet. This, combined with a growing belief in AIS that transformative AI could arrive within years, a real friction is surfaced. Existing fellowships and organizations may hesitate to partner with something that on the surface is concerned with long-horizon research, and funders may conclude that resources are better allocated elsewhere for a more immediate payoff. This friction is perhaps misplaced, as SIRA’s output can address both short- and long-term risk.

I believe that Anthropic’s public position on timelines is instructive here. Despite Anthropic being a short-timeline focused organization, they still fund this type of work. Instead of betting on one timeline, they hedge many bets across several scenarios (optimistic, intermediate, pessimistic), and they structure their research so impact holds value regardless of which scenario turns out to be true.[^15]

When we apply this logic to societal impacts research, specifically through [Anthropic’s Societal Impacts Team](https://www.anthropic.com/research/team/societal-impacts) and the newer [Anthropic Institute](https://www.anthropic.com/institute), it is clear that this work has to happen concurrently with capability development, rather than retroactively or in a constant game of catch-up, as in the case of the Industrial Revolution. If timelines *are* short, society potentially has *less* time to adapt and understand the effects of AI. By this logic, concurrent societal impacts research of AI engenders more value per unit of time.

### The counterarguments, and why they’re worth taking seriously

The strongest objection to societal impact research is about opportunity cost. Much of AIS treats technical alignment as one of the central bottlenecks on existential risk.[^16] Technical alignment work is important, and nothing here is meant to argue otherwise. But insofar as some grant makers and organizations weigh it as the dominant lever, hours spent on societal-impact research, however valuable in its own right, can look to them like hours not spent on the thing they believe matters most directly.

The second objection is about tractability under time pressure. This fellowship is not a fieldbuilding program. It is a research fellowship with a mission to identify promising individuals, support their development through mentored research at the intersection of the social sciences and AIS, and connect them with the broader AIS ecosystem.

# Appendix

## Anticipated Questions

Early feedback on this doc raised the questions below; I have done my best to concisely answer them.

**What does “social science” mean here?**  
Social science refers to empirical research on humans and institutions using established methods (experiments, surveys, interviews, ethnography, quantitative modeling of behavior and institutions) applied to AI systems and their effects, grounded in viewing AI as embedded in larger sociotechnical systems. This is distinct from purely technical/mathematical alignment work and from purely legal/regulatory policy work.

**What sub-disciplines do we prioritize?**  
Based on where the field is already finding traction: psychology (trust calibration, mental models of AI, human-AI interaction, science of personas, AI system behavior), economics (incentive effects, labor market, adoption dynamics), and sociology (institutional capacity, governance effects, technology embeddedness). We recognize that by prioritizing certain sub-disciplines, we inadvertently marginalize others. We do not wish to be purely exclusive to the above sub-disciplines, but we believe having a focus can help clarify where the fellowship can make the most impact. Outside of these sub-disciplines, we welcome researchers from communication (theoretical and applied), anthropology, cognitive science, and linguistics.

**What is the difference between the social sciences and policy/governance work?**  
Policy/governance research generally asks “what rules should govern this?” They design regulation, evaluate compliance mechanisms, and advise decision-makers. Social science research generally asks “what is actually happening?” They design empirical studies of how humans and institutions reason about, trust, use, and are reshaped by AI systems in practice. Of course, at the convergence of these two is a murky middle ground. This fellowship hopes to address questions squarely focused on the social sciences, rather than through a policy/governance lens, given there are already many fellowships doing that type of research.

**What is the potential impact of this work?**  
We see the impact of this work falling under two categories. (1) Near-term: producing the measurement infrastructure that catches sociotechnical failure modes before they scale (e.g., distributional harms, harmful human-AI feedback loops, preference manipulation via recommender/interaction design, and information-environment degradation). (2) Long-term: building the empirical base needed to detect early signs of epistemic security erosion, gradual disempowerment, and power concentration. This is also the kind of human-behavior data interpretability and alignment evaluations need to be validated against, for example, technical safety claims about “understanding and steering model behavior” should require empirical testing in real human-AI interaction settings.

**What is the theory of change?**  
If social scientists are given a structured path into AIS through mentored research, then they will produce relevant AIS research during the program and successfully transition into impactful careers in the field. For a full breakdown, see [Theory of Change](#theory-of-change).

**What are the ethics of these types of studies, specifically for human participants?**  
We hope that some fellows will pursue projects that involve human participants. As such, an IRB will be mandatory.

**How does social impacts research connect to specific threat models?**  
AI-enabled persuasion and manipulation (erosion of epistemic autonomy), miscalibrated trust in AI outputs (over- or under-reliance/automation bias), gradual disempowerment (declining effective human oversight as more decision authority is delegated to AI systems), power concentration (AI widening capability asymmetries between actors), and structural/race-dynamics risk (competitive deployment pressure eroding safety norms industry-wide).

## Illustrative Research Questions {#illustrative-research-questions}

One way to show this fellowship produces work relevant to both short and long timelines, not just the long-horizon institution-building emphasized elsewhere in this doc, is to show the actual research questions it could take on. Below is a starting list, split by timeline.

Short Timelines

* Economics: What incentive structures drive frontier labs to underinvest in safety under competitive racing dynamics, and what near-term market interventions, such as compute-sharing agreements or insurance markets for AI liability, could shift those incentives within the next few years?  
* Economics/Political Economy: How does near-term labor market disruption from AI deployment affect public and political willingness to support AI safety regulation before the disruption itself destabilizes the coalitions needed to pass it?  
* Psychology: As AI systems become the default aid for everyday judgment and decision-making, how does that shift human epistemic autonomy and vulnerability to manipulation?  
* Psychology: What psychological factors, such as motivated reasoning, groupthink, or status-quo bias, predict which developers, researchers, or policymakers underweight catastrophic risk, and how does that shape safety culture inside labs currently racing to deploy?  
* Sociology: Drawing on the sociology of organizational disaster, how do AI labs structurally normalize risk over time in ways that could produce a near-term catastrophic failure, and what organizational interventions reduce that tendency?  
* Communication: How do current AI-generated content and persuasion capabilities degrade the information ecosystem's integrity in ways that could undermine society's ability to coordinate a response to AI risk itself?

Long Timelines

* Economics: In the spirit of "economics of the singularity" work, what happens to long-run growth, interest rates, and capital allocation once AI removes the historical labor-driven ceiling on economic growth, and what risks does that create for wealth concentration and geopolitical stability?  
* Economics: What institutional structures, such as new forms of property rights over AI-generated value or new redistribution mechanisms, would be needed to preserve broad economic participation and political voice once most economically valuable labor is automated?  
* Psychology: Over a multi-generational timescale, how does offloading cognitive and moral judgment to AI systems affect human agency, skill retention, and the capacity for independent moral reasoning, and what does that imply for humanity's ability to oversee increasingly capable AI?  
* Sociology: Using the accumulative risk framework, which slow-moving erosions of societal resilience, such as loss of institutional trust, concentration of infrastructure control, or a degraded epistemic commons, compound over decades to leave civilization vulnerable to a single large shock, and which of these are most tractable to address now?  
* Sociology: What would durable, legitimate global governance institutions for a post-transformative-AI world need to look like, and what does the sociology of prior transnational institution-building, such as nuclear nonproliferation regimes, suggest about their feasibility?  
* Communication: How do societies maintain a shared, trustworthy epistemic commons over decades once AI systems mediate most information production and curation, and what institutional designs preserve democratic legitimacy under that condition?
