Who Gets to Be an Expert? AI, Professions, and the Fight Over Jurisdiction

Emilio Barkett
March 5, 2026

A new bill in New York takes aim at AI chatbots that give legal and medical advice. Sociologists of expertise would not be surprised.

In 2025, New York state Senator Kristen Gonzalez introduced Senate Bill S7263, a short but pointed piece of legislation. The bill would prohibit the owners of AI chatbot systems from allowing those chatbots to provide substantive legal or medical advice—the kind of advice that, if given by a human, would require a professional license. Chatbot owners who violate the rule could face civil lawsuits from users. The bill recently advanced out of the Senate’s Internet and Technology Committee on a unanimous 6–0 vote, and its logic is straightforward: AI should not be permitted to do what doctors and lawyers do.

But why draw that line now, and why draw it there? One way to make sense of this legislation is through Andrew Abbott’s influential theory of professional jurisdiction, developed in his 1988 book The System of Professions. Abbott’s central argument is that what defines a profession is not its credentials or its ethics codes, but its claim to exclusive authority over a particular domain of work—what he calls a “jurisdiction.” As he puts it bluntly in his introduction, “the central phenomenon of professional life is thus the link between a profession and its work.” Professions do not simply exist; they compete within a system.

Medicine claims the diagnosis and treatment of illness. Law claims legal counsel and representation. These claims are never settled once and for all—they are constantly contested, defended, and renegotiated as new actors enter the scene. Abbott argues that earlier scholarship went badly wrong by studying professions one at a time while ignoring interprofessional competition, which he describes as a fundamental feature of professional life.

What makes a profession durable in this competitive system? Abbott’s answer is abstraction: the ability to reframe practical problems in terms of a formal knowledge system. A profession survives when it can translate everyday problems into technical categories governed by specialized knowledge. This capacity allows professions to redefine tasks, defend them from competitors, and expand into new areas.

This is also what makes professions newly vulnerable to AI. Large language models are tools for synthesizing and applying knowledge at scale. Someone who asks a chatbot whether their landlord violated a lease agreement, or whether two medications interact safely, receives something that resembles expert advice without consulting a licensed professional. From the perspective of the legal and medical professions, this is not merely a technological development. It represents a jurisdictional incursion.

S7263 can be interpreted as a legislative response to exactly this threat. By prohibiting chatbots from giving advice that “if taken by a natural person, would constitute a crime” under professional licensing law, the bill effectively attempts to reinscribe existing jurisdictional boundaries onto new technological terrain. It states, in effect, that these domains belong to licensed professionals regardless of whether a machine can perform similar tasks.

Thomas Gieryn’s concept of boundary-work adds a second layer to this interpretation. Writing in 1983, Gieryn argued that the demarcation of science from non-science is not simply a philosophical question but an ongoing social process. Professionals actively construct boundaries in order to distinguish legitimate expertise from competing forms of knowledge. Boundary-work involves attributing specific characteristics to a field in order to define what counts as legitimate practice and what does not.

These boundaries are strategic and context dependent. Gieryn shows that actors emphasize different features of expertise depending on the opponent they are trying to exclude. Scientific authority might be described as empirical when contrasted with religion, or theoretical when contrasted with craft knowledge. The defining characteristics shift depending on who the challenger is.

This flexibility is visible in the logic of S7263. The legislation does not argue that AI-generated advice is necessarily inaccurate. Instead, it asserts that the act of giving legal or medical advice belongs exclusively to licensed professionals. The prohibition targets the activity rather than the quality of the output. That distinction makes more sense when viewed through the lens of jurisdictional defense rather than consumer protection alone.

This perspective also clarifies the tension at the center of the bill. Legislators present the measure as a consumer protection effort, and that concern is plausible. AI-generated advice could plausibly harm users who rely on it. But boundary-work often serves both public and professional interests simultaneously. Protecting professional jurisdiction and protecting public safety are not mutually exclusive motivations.

Abbott himself emphasized that jurisdictional claims are not merely self-interested attempts at monopoly. Professional authority often reflects real differences in training, accountability, and social responsibility. The broader point is that debates over AI and professional expertise are not simply technical debates. They are also struggles over who has the legitimate authority to perform particular kinds of work.

Abbott concluded The System of Professions with a prescient observation: that computer systems capable of abstract reasoning would force professions to defend their jurisdictions in new ways. That prediction now appears remarkably accurate. S7263 represents one of the first legislative attempts to confront this challenge directly.

Whether the bill succeeds or not, it marks a significant moment in the evolving relationship between technological capability and professional authority. The system of professions is being tested—and, as Abbott anticipated, it is responding by defending its boundaries.
